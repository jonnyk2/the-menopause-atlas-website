The Menopause Atlas doesn't tell you what to take. It tells you what the evidence says, so you can walk in and have the conversation you came for.

How it is different

Built to be used in the exam room, not admired on the shelf.

Three rules run through all 424 pages. They are what make this a reference rather than another opinion.

RULE ONE

Real numbers, not scary percentages.

A headline says hormone therapy "raises breast cancer risk 26%." This book shows what that means per thousand women over five years, next to the number for women taking nothing, so you can judge the size of the risk for yourself.

RULE TWO

Sources in the sentence.

Studies are named where the claim is made, with the journal and the year, and every evidence box says what kind of study it was and where it is weak. When two good studies disagree, you get both and a plain account of why.

RULE THREE

It never tells you what to take.

No doses, no regimens. Where a treatment needs a prescription, the book explains what it is for, what the trials found, and what to ask. Nearly every chapter ends with questions for your clinician and the red flags that mean don't wait.

CHAPTER 19 · WHY ROUTE MATTERS

"No hormone therapy" and "no oral hormone therapy" are different answers.

In absolute terms the risk is small. Even at the higher estimates from the Cochrane review, about 99 in 100 women taking oral combined therapy for a year will not have a clot. It is not the coin flip it can sound like.

A Swedish nationwide registry study of over 900,000 women, published in the BMJ in 2024, compared eight hormone therapy routes and regimens. Every oral form raised clot risk. Transdermal forms, tracked as separate groups, did not.

One sentence to bring to your clinician: if you were told you cannot take hormone therapy because of clot risk, ask whether that applies to transdermal estrogen too, or only to the pill. Some clotting conditions carry risk whatever the route, so this is a conversation for your clinician, not a self-diagnosis. Have it anyway.

Who wrote it

Thea E. Kuiper

You are entitled to know who is speaking before you decide how much weight to give it.

The author is not a physician, a nurse, a pharmacist, or a licensed clinician of any kind. Where the book says "no trial I could find," that is a reader of the literature reporting what the literature says, not a clinical opinion, and the book never pretends otherwise. What it is instead is a work of research and synthesis, built on a method you can judge for yourself.

  • Claims are traced to sources, named in the text where the claim is made rather than in an unread list at the back.
  • Evidence is graded, and the grade is printed. Every science box names the study type and its limitations.
  • Absolute risk before relative risk. Where a number can be expressed as how many women in a thousand, it is.
  • Uncertainty is stated, not smoothed over. Where the evidence conflicts, both sides. Where it runs out, a sentence saying so.
  • Drug information is checked against current labels, not summaries of them.
  • Decisions are handed back. Wherever an answer depends on one woman's body, the book says so and gives her the question to ask.
Questions

Before you buy.

Is it for perimenopause too, or only after periods stop?

Both. Most symptoms peak during perimenopause, the years before your final period, and the book is organized around that. It also covers early, premature, and surgical menopause, and the long-term health picture after menopause.

Is this medical advice?

No. It is information, designed to help you talk to a clinician rather than replace one. Nothing in it should be used to start, stop, or change a prescription, supplement, or over-the-counter product on your own.

Is it up to date?

It is current to September 2026 and covers two changes that happened while it was being written: the FDA's removal of risk statements from the hormone therapy boxed warning, and ACOG's 2026 update on evaluating postmenopausal bleeding. The digital edition is corrected when guidance changes, and buyers receive each corrected edition free.

By email, within about a minute of purchase, as a download link for a PDF that opens on any phone, tablet, or computer. There is no DRM, so it opens in whatever reader you already use, including the Kindle and Apple Books apps.

Does it recommend supplements or alternative medicine?
